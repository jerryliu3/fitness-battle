# Overtaek — brand strategy and ten creative territories

Archived first-phase exploration · October 17, 2026. See [the follow-up studio](brand-and-ember-studio.md) for the subsequent preference for Chain Reaction / Play Forward and neutral systems with personal Ember finishes.

## The strategic recommendation

Make **the closing gap** the central brand idea. Overtaek is an unusually useful name: it already contains anticipation, another person, movement, and a change of position. Build recognition around that emotional event rather than around generic fitness improvement.

The proposed master line is **Find your next overtake.** The plain-language descriptor is **Weekly fitness competition.** The proposed lead campaign is **Close the gap.** These serve different jobs: invitation, comprehension, and attention.

Positioning draft:

> For everyday athletes who find another gear when there is someone to chase, Overtaek turns weekly movement into a competition worth coming back to—making effort visible and giving progress a social, sporting payoff.

This is a positioning hypothesis, not validated customer research. The assumed initial audience is recreationally competitive adults and their existing crews, across running, riding, walking, and strength. Different sports should appear across the campaign library; launch advertisements can still focus on a specific audience.

The product context supports weekly asynchronous competition, crews, visible lanes and standings, prior-self comparison, and benchmarks. Keep marketing specific to the release being advertised. In particular, a cinematic pass is a metaphor for changing standings, not a promise of real-time physical racing or an available head-to-head mode.

### Refine the three pillars

1. **Progress gives competition its purpose.** The win should mean more than a number on a leaderboard. Make small gains, returns, consistency, and changed positions worthy of a story. Avoid implying that scoring literally measures percentage improvement or makes every athlete equally likely to win unless the rules establish it.
2. **The overtake is the moment.** Anticipation → closing distance → a change of position → release. This can drive composition, film, motion, copy, sound, and social storytelling.
3. **Rivals make us better.** The other person matters because they draw something out of us. Celebrate a worthy rival alongside the person who passes them. Defeat is a temporary position, not a judgment of character.

Personality: ambitious, alert, generous, a little cheeky. Athletic credibility without intimidation. Warmth without removing the competitive edge.

Voice examples: “Someone worth chasing.” “One place closer.” “Your week is still in play.” “They brought out your best.” Avoid “Destroy your friends,” “No excuses,” guilt about rest, and promises that every workout must improve your rank. “You passed Alex” is appropriate only when the event actually happened.

### What research suggests—and what is interpretation

[Strava](https://www.strava.com/) foregrounds its athlete community and tools such as segments and challenges. [Nike Run Club](https://www.nike.com/nrc-app) combines running support, coaching, and community challenges. [Tracksmith](https://help.tracksmith.com/en-US/who-are-we-415087) explicitly champions competitive non-professionals and the amateur spirit. These brands demonstrate that community, improvement, and amateur competition are established territory. My interpretation is that Overtaek needs a more specific recurring story: the people close enough to chase and the moment you gain ground. This is a limited category scan, not evidence that no other brand uses that idea.

## What decides the brand

Decide these in order:

1. **Audience and ambition:** whose identity are we helping express? “Everyday athlete who enjoys a chase” is more useful than “anyone interested in fitness.”
2. **Emotional promise:** anticipation, belonging, playful pressure, achievement, or shared momentum? Choose a primary emotion; the others can support it.
3. **Credible reason:** weekly competition, visible progress, crews, and a repeatable sporting rhythm. Emotion needs product evidence.
4. **Distinctive assets:** a mark, a graphic behavior, a typographic voice, a photographic point of view, and recurring language people can recognize together.
5. **Expression:** palette, lettering, cropping, materials, motion, sound, and the relationship between people and graphics.
6. **Applications and operating cost:** will it work on a phone icon, a street poster, a quiet result card, a creator video, a web hero, and a one-color shirt? Can a small team keep making it well?

Colors come after the promise. Their role matters more than a list of hex values: establish the base field, text color, attention signal, secondary support, and image treatment. Test recognition both in full color and with the signal color removed. Typography should work with “Overtaek,” “OVERTAEK,” “overtaek,” numerals, and short verbs before judging an alphabet specimen.

“Overtaek” is pronounced like “overtake.” Test whether people remember and reproduce the spelling after seeing or hearing it. A custom a/e relationship is a possible wordmark opportunity, not a reason to distort the name. Wordmarks and symbols shown in the sketches are studies, not finished identity assets.

## Ten creative territories

Palette order below is described explicitly; these are exploration values rather than approved accessibility or print specifications. Font pairings are starting points; final selection needs real specimen testing. The accompanying visual review shows original typography and graphic sketches, not complete photographic moodboards or finished App Store submissions.

### 01 — The Pass

**Belief:** the most motivating distance is the one you can close. **Feeling:** focused anticipation followed by release. **Audience pull:** a competitive recreational athlete who wants sport to feel consequential without adopting elite-athlete theatre.

**Aesthetic:** modern sporting precision. Deep racing green `#15251E`, electric chartreuse `#C4F54F`, optic paper `#F3F5EC`, field grey `#778576`. Barlow Condensed for compressed, decisive headlines; DM Sans for readable explanations. A custom wordmark can introduce a small passing offset while keeping the spelling effortless to read.

**Visual signature:** two lanes that exchange the lead; an offset cut that recurs in images, type, and framing. Photograph two people from shoulder height or close to the ground, with one entering the other's frame. Use ordinary athletes, strength sessions, walking groups, and bikes as well as runners. The expressive device is the change in relationship, not a generic speed streak.

**Headline:** “Close the gap.” Supporting invitation: “Find your next overtake.”

**Same five contexts:**
- Poster: huge compressed headline interrupted by two passing lanes; one human detail rather than a full-body stock sprint.
- App Store sequence: “Make your move” → visible weekly standings → crews → personal progress. Attach each claim to an actual release capture.
- Instagram: “One place closer” result story using a real change of position; the same offset marks frame a portrait on the next post.
- Website: a passing transition introduces the headline, then immediately explains weekly fitness competition. It must work as a still.
- App icon: two unmistakably offset paths, drawn to survive a small size and one-color reproduction.

**Motion/sound:** one controlled crossover and a brief two-part rising cue. No permanent motion trails. **Risk:** could resemble generic sports-tech branding if reduced to green, italics, and stripes. The passing relationship and human casting must carry recognition. **Assessment:** strongest master-brand candidate; clearest connection between name, feeling, and graphic system.

### 02 — After Hours

**Belief:** your athletic life does not end where the working day does. **Feeling:** electricity, city belonging, a second wind. **Audience pull:** time-constrained city athletes and crews who fit movement around real life.

**Aesthetic:** cobalt `#162AFA` as the dominant field, coral `#FF7262` as light and interruption, cool white `#F4F5FF`, midnight `#0D1548`. Syne display type paired with DM Sans. Tilted compositions, direct flash, long exposure, reflective fabrics, cropped street furniture, and imperfect human moments.

**Visual signature:** a light trace connecting one person's movement to the next. It is urban sporting culture, not a futuristic nightclub interface.

**Headline:** “Your day isn't done.”

**Same five contexts:**
- Poster: bus-shelter-scale cobalt with a flash-lit training pair and a short tilted line.
- App Store: “Your hours. Your move” → asynchronous weekly competition → crew momentum, with clear real screens.
- Instagram: an after-work athlete series; what the day demanded and what they still found time to do.
- Website: documentary mini-films spanning commute, training, and the next morning.
- App icon: a bold interrupted light-loop O; retain a readable silhouette without glow.

**Motion/sound:** quick camera cuts and a restrained percussive rhythm. **Risk:** excludes morning, rural, older, and indoor audiences if “after hours” becomes literal night-only imagery. **Assessment:** strong launch campaign or city-focused brand; narrower universal master story than The Pass.

### 03 — Rival Club

**Belief:** a worthy rival is a form of belonging. **Feeling:** sporting pride, mutual respect, membership. **Audience pull:** crews and recreational athletes who want a shared identity worth wearing.

**Aesthetic:** oxblood `#601B35`, warm parchment `#FFF6D8`, sporting blue `#3856B9`, muted brass `#D7B775`. Fraunces brings character and editorial confidence; DM Sans keeps supporting information contemporary. Clean club stamps and editorial rules rather than a pile of fake heritage crests.

**Visual signature:** paired portraits of people who bring out each other's best; a recurring contemporary club stamp; weekly honours that tell a story. Friends can compete without becoming enemies.

**Headline:** “Good rivals. Better athletes.”

**Same five contexts:**
- Poster: one pair of portraits, a strong serif line, and one club identifier.
- App Store: “Make a rival of a friend” → Crew evidence → weekly standings → benchmark progress.
- Instagram: The Rivalry Register, a repeatable series of two people and the effort they inspire in each other.
- Website: a club editorial with people and rivalries before a clear product explanation.
- App icon: a simplified O stamp, independent of any fine crest lettering.

**Motion/sound:** alternating portraits, passing a club identifier, conversational athlete voices. **Risk:** can look inherited, exclusive, or like an apparel brand; Tracksmith's existing amateur positioning makes a generic heritage treatment weak differentiation. **Assessment:** best culture and merchandise potential, provided it feels like a new club anyone can join.

### 04 — Good Pressure

**Belief:** a little friendly rivalry helps you discover more of yourself. **Feeling:** cheekiness, encouragement, delight. **Audience pull:** people drawn to competition but put off by aggressive sports identities.

**Aesthetic:** lilac `#DFCFFA`, dark aubergine `#30134A`, hot orange `#FB582C`, warm white `#FFF8EF`. Rounded, dense DM Sans display settings as a prototype for custom expressive lettering; the same family reads cleanly in supporting text. Soft forms compress, nudge, and stretch. Candid photography includes effort faces and the laugh afterwards.

**Visual signature:** two soft forms push each other into a new shape. Personality comes from social interaction rather than a cute mascot pasted on every asset.

**Headline:** “A little rivalry. A lot more you.”

**Same five contexts:**
- Poster: bold lilac, one orange interruption, expressive friendly headline, two people reacting to each other.
- App Store: “A nudge worth having” → actual crew activity → competition → visible progress.
- Instagram: “Your friend made you do it,” with the athlete's own funny account of showing up.
- Website: a welcoming invitation with visible competitive proof directly below it.
- App icon: an ownable soft passing-loop study; distinguish it from generic circular wellness marks.

**Motion/sound:** a gentle press, a spring forward, a short human-feeling bounce cue. **Risk:** looks like wellness or a habit app if there is no visible sporting tension. **Assessment:** strongest accessible alternative; needs confident competitive language to retain Overtaek's reason to exist.

### 05 — Marginal Gains

**Belief:** progress becomes motivating when you can see its shape. **Feeling:** clarity, mastery, satisfaction. **Audience pull:** curious, methodical athletes who enjoy understanding their own effort.

**Aesthetic:** deep petrol `#083C43`, mint `#72E8BE`, pale field `#E7FAEF`, mineral grey `#799B9B`. Space Grotesk for human technical confidence; IBM Plex Mono for small measurement annotations. Physical test sheets, close-up materials, rulers, deliberate negative space.

**Visual signature:** measurement ticks around real human movement; small progress treated as something worth observing. Keep the visual intelligence tactile and comprehensible.

**Headline:** “Small gains. Real ground.”

**Same five contexts:**
- Poster: an athlete's hand or shoe, one measured relationship, one short line.
- App Store: “See the gap. Make your move” → real lane values → benchmarks → weekly comparison.
- Instagram: The Progress Report, showing one real change with an athlete's explanation.
- Website: a calm, exact explanation of how a week becomes a competitive story.
- App icon: a cropped measured O with very few marks at small sizes.

**Motion/sound:** measured steps that resolve rather than endlessly count upward. **Risk:** implies coaching, clinical accuracy, or physiological insights the product does not provide; can feel like analytics software. **Assessment:** credible specialist direction, less immediately emotional for broad acquisition.

### 06 — Proof of Work

**Belief:** ordinary effort deserves a visible record. **Feeling:** honesty, dignity, earned confidence. **Audience pull:** people tired of polished transformation imagery and fitness performance theatre.

**Aesthetic:** charcoal `#272525`, uncoated grey-white `#F0EEEA`, fluorescent yellow `#ECF249`, concrete `#95928B`. Archivo Black for blunt statements, IBM Plex Mono for record-like details. Monochrome documentary photography, daylight, real training environments, human texture.

**Visual signature:** an “effort receipt” alongside the person who earned it. The receipt records a legitimate session or week; it is not an invented verification badge. “Proof of Work” is an internal territory name, not a proposed public tagline with cryptocurrency baggage.

**Headline:** “The work shows.”

**Same five contexts:**
- Poster: one unretouched portrait with a narrow fluorescent record strip.
- App Store: “Show up. See it count” → actual counted sessions → standings → personal history.
- Instagram: Effort, On Record: a person, a real week, and why it mattered.
- Website: a documentary athlete series connected to a straightforward product invitation.
- App icon: a forceful one-color stamp, avoiding small receipt details.

**Motion/sound:** hard photographic cuts, paper movement, an understated confirmation. **Risk:** moralizes exercise or romanticizes exhaustion if copy celebrates suffering rather than agency; production depends on good original portraits. **Assessment:** excellent long-term content platform, especially inside a more distinctive master identity.

### 07 — The Weekly Sport

**Belief:** your ordinary training week can have the drama and ritual of sport. **Feeling:** anticipation, relevance, a reason to return. **Audience pull:** people who follow sport and like the narrative of positions, comebacks, and weekly results.

**Aesthetic:** sporting red `#EF3138`, warm white `#FFFDF3`, scoreboard black `#19191C`, silver `#C0C6CB`. Barlow Condensed mastheads and IBM Plex Mono annotations. Bold editorial hierarchy, sporting schedules, recap pages, decisive image crops.

**Visual signature:** a weekly sports front page in which everyday athletes are the protagonists. Present the actual asynchronous week; do not imply live broadcast racing or artificial urgency.

**Headline:** “Your week. Still in play.”

**Same five contexts:**
- Poster: one sports-page headline and one compelling ordinary-athlete moment.
- App Store: “A new week. A new chase” → weekly field → changing standings → crew story.
- Instagram: The Week in Overtaek: recaps, comebacks, close contests, and athlete spotlights.
- Website: this week's sporting story followed by an explanation of how to take part.
- App icon: a compact proprietary sporting letterform, without broadcast-network imitation.

**Motion/sound:** a restrained opening ident and editorial transitions. **Risk:** constant urgency, fake “breaking news,” or professional-sport parody. **Assessment:** strongest recurring campaign engine because the product itself supplies the weekly publishing rhythm.

### 08 — Higher Ground

**Belief:** other people help you discover a wider horizon for yourself. **Feeling:** possibility, perspective, sustained ambition. **Audience pull:** athletes motivated by growth and a long view rather than a constant adrenaline hit.

**Aesthetic:** forest `#183A30`, glacial blue `#9CDDE7`, pale mist `#E6F1EE`, lichen `#B5BFA3`. Fraunces editorial headlines with Space Grotesk support. Wide, breathable photography, rising horizons, topographic crops, human scale.

**Visual signature:** a horizon that moves upward as the person moves forward. Show stairs, streets, gyms, walking routes, and everyday terrain as well as spectacular landscapes.

**Headline:** “There is more in you.”

**Same five contexts:**
- Poster: a person and a clear horizon with enough negative space to feel expansive.
- App Store: “See how far you can go” → personal benchmarks → weekly competition → peers.
- Instagram: Further, Together: the person who helped someone believe they could do more.
- Website: immersive still imagery with a grounded, plain product story.
- App icon: an open horizon O that remains simple without landscape detail.

**Motion/sound:** a slow rising reveal and open, spacious sound. **Risk:** mistaken for an outdoor travel, meditation, or trail-only product. **Assessment:** emotionally generous and premium, but weaker at immediately signaling the competitive mechanism.

### 09 — Play Forward

**Belief:** movement becomes compelling when effort produces a satisfying next move. **Feeling:** play, agency, progression. **Audience pull:** people who enjoy games, visible accomplishment, and expressive digital culture.

**Aesthetic:** magenta `#F941A1`, dark violet `#251346`, acid lime `#CBF14F`, chalk lavender `#F8F1FF`. Syne display type and Space Grotesk support. Tactile dimensional steps, cropped position changes, graphic tokens grounded in real athletes.

**Visual signature:** a physical-looking step forward, representing an actual achievement. The product remains a fitness competition; dimensional art must not imply a different playable game or unreleased reward system.

**Headline:** “Real effort. Next level.”

**Same five contexts:**
- Poster: one bold dimensional step with a real athlete interacting with its composition.
- App Store: “Put your week in play” → actual division and lane views → real achievements.
- Instagram: expressive graphic celebrations attached to truthful weekly events.
- Website: one satisfying position-change moment, followed by a clear fitness explanation.
- App icon: a strong dimensional passing symbol with an equally clear flat version.

**Motion/sound:** tactile movement and brief celebratory resolution. **Risk:** childlike gaming or a promise of a game that the app cannot deliver. **Assessment:** strongest home for a leading mascot and playful achievement system; a deliberate commitment, not decorative gamification.

### 10 — Chain Reaction

**Belief:** one person's effort changes what the people around them do next. **Feeling:** connection, influence, collective momentum. **Audience pull:** socially motivated athletes who show up because someone they know does.

**Aesthetic:** golden yellow `#F8CE43`, dark plum `#342344`, warm paper `#FFFBEA`, coral `#F46D50`. DM Sans with custom linked lettering as a future identity study. Connected circles, relay compositions, split-frame group photography, warm natural light.

**Visual signature:** one movement continues into another person's frame. Unlike Rival Club's membership or Good Pressure's friendly nudge, this story is about the ripple outward through a group.

**Headline:** “Your move moves someone.”

**Same five contexts:**
- Poster: a chain of people or gestures with a single linking graphic.
- App Store: “Your crew. More momentum” → actual crew progress → competition → individual contribution.
- Instagram: Pass the Momentum, using consenting crew stories and real activity rather than invented causal claims.
- Website: a sequence of people whose participation encourages one another.
- App icon: a tightly simplified linked O; no tiny network of nodes.

**Motion/sound:** a relay cut from one athlete to another, with a short accumulating rhythm. **Risk:** becomes generic community software if rivalry disappears. **Assessment:** strongest referral and crew invitation story; likely a supporting campaign unless the crew is the primary acquisition wedge.

## How to choose

### Recommended shortlist

**The Pass** is the leading master-brand recommendation. It makes the name do work, turns the product's emotional payoff into a repeatable graphic behavior, and can operate across all four sports. Its proximity to the existing green/tennis product palette makes adoption practical, but that is not the argument for choosing it.

**Rival Club** is the alternative if the ambition is a sporting culture people want to belong to and wear. It asks for consistent human storytelling and a distinctive contemporary club language.

**Good Pressure** is the alternative if intimidation is the main acquisition barrier. It lets Overtaek remain competitive while making the social experience feel appealing and approachable.

Do not assemble the final identity from one territory's palette, another's crest, another's typography, and another's 3D objects. Choose a dominant visual world. Respectful rivalry and honest product proof should apply to every world.

The Weekly Sport, Proof of Work, and Chain Reaction are particularly useful campaign platforms, even if their visual identities are not selected. Carry over the editorial idea only after translating it into the chosen master system.

### Board types and deliverables

- **Strategy sheet:** audience, promise, positioning, pillars, personality, and proof. One concise page.
- **Vision board per finalist:** roughly 10–15 carefully selected images across people, lighting, type, materials, composition, motion, and cultural references. Caption what is relevant. Include references from outside fitness; avoid copying one incumbent's complete world.
- **Concept board per finalist:** original palette roles, wordmark/mark studies, headline specimens, one graphic behavior, photo direction, a motion storyboard, and the same five contextual applications. This turn supplies early typographic/graphic sketches for all ten, not ten fully produced photographic boards.
- **Final brand board:** the selected identity distilled into a page someone else can reliably use. It is a conclusion of exploration, not a substitute for it.
- **Working brand guide:** reusable templates, crop rules, voice examples, content series, motion/sound rules, accessibility pairings, and production/export guidance.

### Fair contextual tests

Use the same deliverables and comparable fidelity for all three finalists:

1. **Street poster:** one dominant message, one visual idea, recognizability at a glance. Review a clean flat design and a scaled context image. Avoid making a weak idea seem stronger through a beautiful wall mockup.
2. **App Store sequence:** first frame makes weekly fitness competition clear; next frames demonstrate the chase, the people, and progress with actual release captures. Use benefit-led headlines, not the same vague slogan on every frame. Test frames independently as well as in sequence.
3. **Instagram system:** a portrait story, a result/progress post, and a campaign message. The brand must remain recognizable when the subject, copy length, and crop change.
4. **Website:** hero, a useful explanation of the competition, human evidence, and an obvious next action. Test the static first impression before adding motion.
5. **App icon:** large, small, grayscale, and surrounded by other icons. It must work without the wordmark or a decorative rendering effect.

[Apple's asset guidance](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/asset-best-practices/) emphasizes a clear focal point, short legible copy, coherent brand assets, and recognizable icons. Its [app preview guidance](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/app-previews/) specifically calls for footage of the app in use. These are separate from the concept's poster-like visual studies. Finished store assets must show the actual product appropriately; conceptual graphic art is not a replacement for truthful product demonstration.

### Evaluation method

Use a directional founder/design scorecard: strategic fit 25%, distinctive recognition 25%, desired emotional response 20%, cross-context usefulness 15%, repeatable production 15%. These weights are proposed decision priorities, not research findings. Separately require truthful product representation and readable execution; an attractive aggregate score cannot compensate for failing either.

Recruit approximately 12–18 people for an initial qualitative round across people who already compete socially, people who want motivation but find competition intimidating, and crew organizers. This is for learning and eliminating weak directions, not statistically proving a winner. Randomize exposure order, show equal-quality assets, and ask before explaining the concept:

- After five seconds: what is this, who is it for, and what would it let you do?
- What three words describe how it feels? What makes you say that?
- Does this make you want to join, chase someone, bring a friend, or avoid it? Why?
- After a short unrelated task, which graphic detail and name do you remember?
- Which assets appear to come from the same brand when the logo is covered?
- Does any direction make you expect features the product does not actually have?

Only then ask which they prefer. Designer admiration is different from comprehension, recall, or motivation. Advance a direction with the strongest combination, then use larger live acquisition tests when real product pages, release assets, and adequate traffic are available. Do not mistake a tiny split test for a conversion conclusion.

## Recommended first expression

Start by developing **The Pass** into a complete identity with the following creative brief:

> Overtaek is the weekly fitness competition that gives you someone worth chasing. Make closing the gap feel thrilling, possible, and human. Build the identity around a passing relationship that remains recognizable in one color, in a still image, and without the logo. Cast everyday athletes with credible effort and mutual respect. Put the chase on the poster and the proof in the product imagery.

Wordmark: OVERTAEK as the decisive headline signature; test a more human mixed-case form beside it. Primary graphic asset: two offset lanes exchanging the lead. Photography: real people in relation to one another. Voice: short, sporting, generous. Core descriptor: Weekly fitness competition. Master invitation: Find your next overtake. Campaign: Close the gap.

Keep the identity sport-neutral. A runner-only launch asset is legitimate; a runner-only brand library is limiting. Let an existing mascot play a supporting role unless a deliberately playful territory wins. Build the icon from the core symbol rather than from a detailed mascot illustration by default.

The decision still to validate is emotional: **does the audience most want a chase, a club, or a friendly push?** The finalist boards and contextual tests should answer that question before the palette becomes a permanent commitment.
